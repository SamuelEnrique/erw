# ERCOT: the written layer

## What this grid is and who runs it

The Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT) runs the power grid for most of Texas. It is a grid operator, not a utility: it owns no power plants and no power lines. It schedules the plants that others own, keeps supply equal to demand every second, and runs the wholesale market where power is bought and sold. The Public Utility Commission of Texas (PUCT) and the Texas Legislature oversee it. Parts of Texas, near El Paso, in the Panhandle and in the east, belong to other grids.

Sources: https://www.ercot.com/about ; https://www.ercot.com/about/profile ; https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/delivery-to-consumers.php

## How its market sets prices

ERCOT runs two markets. In the day-ahead market, buyers and sellers agree today on power for each hour of tomorrow. In the real-time market, ERCOT dispatches plants every few minutes and pays the price at that moment. Prices are set at each location on the grid (locational marginal prices), so power costs more where lines are congested. A hub, such as the Hub Average on this page, averages many locations into one price that traders use. ERCOT has no capacity market: generators are paid only for the energy they sell and some reliability services, so prices during scarce hours can rise very high, which is meant to reward being available.

Sources: https://www.ercot.com/mktinfo ; https://www.ercot.com/about/profile ; https://www.eia.gov/electricity/wholesale/

## What makes it different

ERCOT is an island. Its grid has only a few small links to the rest of the country and to Mexico, so it cannot lean on its neighbors in an emergency, and because its power does not cross state lines in a big way, the federal regulator (FERC) does not set its market rules. It is also the energy-only market described above. Texas produces more wind electricity than any other state (EIA, https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/wind/where-wind-power-is-harnessed.php) and is adding large amounts of solar and batteries, so the mix on this page changes a lot between a windy night and a sunny afternoon.

Sources: https://www.ercot.com/about/profile ; https://www.eia.gov/electricity/gridmonitor/dashboard/electric_overview/balancing_authority/ERCO

## A short history

- **1970:** ERCOT is formed to manage reliability for the Texas grid (ERCOT, https://www.ercot.com/news/mediakit/backgrounder).
- **2010-12-01:** ERCOT starts its nodal market, with a price at each location on the grid instead of a few zones (ERCOT, https://www.ercot.com/files/docs/2011/04/26/ercot_2010_financial_statements.pdf).
- **2021-02-15:** During Winter Storm Uri, ERCOT orders utilities to cut power to customers across the state because too many power plants had failed in the cold (EIA, https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=46836).

Sources: https://www.ercot.com/about ; https://www.ercot.com/about/profile

## Glossary

- **Balancing authority:** the organization that keeps supply and demand equal in an area. EIA reports ERCOT as balancing authority ERCO.
- **Demand:** how much electricity customers are using, in megawatts (MW).
- **Megawatt (MW) and megawatt-hour (MWh):** a megawatt is a rate of power; a megawatt-hour is one megawatt for one hour, an amount of energy.
- **Net generation:** the power plants produce, minus what they use themselves.
- **Day-ahead market:** trades for each hour of tomorrow, agreed today.
- **Real-time market:** the price paid for power at the moment it is delivered.
- **Hub:** an average of many grid locations' prices, used as one reference price.
- **Peak and off-peak:** peak hours are weekday daytime hours when demand is high; the rest are off-peak.
- **Carbon intensity:** the carbon dioxide released per megawatt-hour of electricity, in kilograms of CO2 per MWh.
- **Interconnection queue:** the waiting list of new power plants and batteries asking to connect to the grid.
