# PJM: the written layer

## What this grid is and who runs it

PJM Interconnection runs the largest wholesale electricity market in the United States (PJM, https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm/who-we-are). Its grid covers all or part of thirteen states and the District of Columbia (PJM, https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm/who-we-are), from New Jersey and Pennsylvania west to Illinois and south to Virginia and North Carolina. PJM owns no power plants: it schedules the plants that its members own, keeps supply equal to demand, and plans the high-voltage transmission lines. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) approves its market rules.

Sources: https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm ; https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm/who-we-are ; https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/delivery-to-consumers.php

## How its market sets prices

Like the other large grids, PJM runs a day-ahead market, where power for each hour of tomorrow is bought and sold today, and a real-time market, where plants are dispatched every few minutes. Prices are set at each location on the grid (locational marginal prices), so power costs more where lines are congested. PJM also pays power plants, batteries and customers who can cut their use to be available years ahead, through its capacity market (next section).

Sources: https://www.pjm.com/markets-and-operations/energy ; https://www.eia.gov/electricity/wholesale/

## What makes it different

The capacity auction. Each year PJM runs an auction, the Reliability Pricing Model (RPM), that buys a promise of enough generating capacity for a future delivery year (PJM, https://www.pjm.com/markets-and-operations/rpm). The result sets a price per megawatt per day that every customer's bill pays for, so a high auction price is news across the whole region. PJM also serves Northern Virginia's large and growing cluster of datacenters, which makes it a sharp example of AI's demand for power. PJM's prices are licensed to the ERW for internal use only, so this page does not show them.

Sources: https://www.pjm.com/markets-and-operations/rpm ; https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm/who-we-are

## A short history

- **1927:** Three utilities in Pennsylvania and New Jersey pool their power plants (PJM, https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm/who-we-are); PJM grows from this pool.
- **2007-06-01:** The first delivery year of PJM's capacity market, the Reliability Pricing Model, begins.
- **2022-12-24:** During Winter Storm Elliott, many power plants fail in the cold and PJM calls on emergency procedures to keep the lights on.

Sources: https://www.pjm.com/about-pjm/who-we-are ; https://www.pjm.com/markets-and-operations/rpm

## Glossary

- **Balancing authority:** the organization that keeps supply and demand equal in an area. EIA reports PJM as balancing authority PJM.
- **Demand:** how much electricity customers are using, in megawatts (MW).
- **Megawatt (MW) and megawatt-hour (MWh):** a megawatt is a rate of power; a megawatt-hour is one megawatt for one hour, an amount of energy.
- **Net generation:** the power plants produce, minus what they use themselves.
- **Day-ahead market:** trades for each hour of tomorrow, agreed today.
- **Real-time market:** the price paid for power at the moment it is delivered.
- **Capacity market:** a market that pays resources to be available in a future year, separate from paying for the energy they produce.
- **Reliability Pricing Model (RPM):** PJM's capacity market and its yearly auction.
- **Datacenter:** a building full of computer servers; large ones use as much power as a small city.
- **Carbon intensity:** the carbon dioxide released per megawatt-hour of electricity, in kilograms of CO2 per MWh.

Sources: https://www.eia.gov/electricity/gridmonitor/about ; https://www.pjm.com/markets-and-operations/rpm
