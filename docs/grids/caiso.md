# CAISO: the written layer

## What this grid is and who runs it

The California Independent System Operator (CAISO) runs the high-voltage grid for most of California and a small part of Nevada. It is a nonprofit grid operator: it owns no power plants, schedules the plants that others own, keeps supply equal to demand, and runs the wholesale market. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) approves its market rules, and California's energy agencies set the state's energy policy. Some California utilities, such as the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power and the Sacramento Municipal Utility District, run their own balancing areas outside CAISO.

Sources: https://www.caiso.com/about ; https://www.caiso.com/about/our-business ; https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/delivery-to-consumers.php

## How its market sets prices

CAISO runs a day-ahead market, where power for each hour of tomorrow is bought and sold today, and a real-time market, where plants are dispatched every few minutes at the price of that moment. Prices are set at each location (locational marginal prices). Trading hubs summarize them: NP15 in the north, ZP26 in the middle and SP15 in the south; this page uses SP15. CAISO also runs a real-time market for utilities across the West, which lets them trade power with California every few minutes.

Sources: https://www.caiso.com/about/our-business ; https://www.westernenergymarkets.com/ ; https://www.eia.gov/electricity/wholesale/

## What makes it different

Sunshine. California produces more solar electricity than any other state (EIA, https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/solar/where-solar-is-found.php), so in the middle of a sunny day the grid can have more power than it needs: prices fall, sometimes below zero, and some solar is turned off (curtailed). When the sun sets, solar output drops just as people come home, and other plants and batteries must ramp up quickly. This evening ramp is why California has built so many batteries, which charge at midday and discharge in the evening: you can see that daily cycle in the battery block on this page.

Sources: https://www.caiso.com/todays-outlook ; https://www.eia.gov/electricity/gridmonitor/dashboard/electric_overview/balancing_authority/CISO

## A short history

- **1998-03-31:** CAISO begins operating the grid, after California restructured its electricity industry (CAISO, https://www.caiso.com/documents/rtofiling.pdf).
- **2014-11-01:** The Western Energy Imbalance Market starts, letting utilities outside California trade power with CAISO in real time (CAISO, https://www.caiso.com/content/monthly-market-performance/dec-2022/western-energy-imbalance-market.html).
- **2020-08-14:** During a West-wide heat wave, CAISO orders rotating outages because demand outran the power available (CAISO, https://www.caiso.com/Documents/Final-Root-Cause-Analysis-Mid-August-2020-Extreme-Heat-Wave.pdf).

Sources: https://www.caiso.com/about ; https://www.westernenergymarkets.com/

## Glossary

- **Balancing authority:** the organization that keeps supply and demand equal in an area. EIA reports CAISO as balancing authority CISO.
- **Demand:** how much electricity customers are using, in megawatts (MW).
- **Megawatt (MW) and megawatt-hour (MWh):** a megawatt is a rate of power; a megawatt-hour is one megawatt for one hour, an amount of energy.
- **Net generation:** the power plants produce, minus what they use themselves.
- **Day-ahead market:** trades for each hour of tomorrow, agreed today.
- **Real-time market:** the price paid for power at the moment it is delivered.
- **Hub:** an average of many grid locations' prices, used as one reference price. SP15 is Southern California's.
- **Curtailment:** turning down solar or wind that could have produced, because the grid cannot use or move the power.
- **Evening ramp:** the fast rise in other generation needed as solar output falls at sunset.
- **Carbon intensity:** the carbon dioxide released per megawatt-hour of electricity, in kilograms of CO2 per MWh.
