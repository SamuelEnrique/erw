== peak premium decisions
Decisions on this page (each with its default and its source on hover) The day is cut into three blocks by the local clock hour in which a 15-minute interval starts: overnight from 21:00 to 12:00, midday from 12:00 to 16:00, peak from 16:00 to 21:00. i Default: These hours on every day of the year, weekends and holidays included. It is not the industry's on-peak block (the 16 hours from 06:00 to 22:00 on weekdays), so no traded on-peak price compares with these figures. Source: The thesis the chart was built for; docs/methods/ercot_peak_premium.md, "Time-of-day blocks". ERCOT's market monitor places the tightest hours at about 4 to 5 p.m. in the past and at 7 to 9 p.m. now (Potomac Economics, 2025 State of the Market Report for the ERCOT Electricity Markets, May 2026, page 5): both are inside the peak block. The peak premium is read as two figures: the peak block's median less the midday block's median, and the peak block's interquartile range (its Q3 less its Q1). i Default: Medians and quartiles, not means. No mean is on this page: a mean is moved by a few very high intervals (at the hub average in 2021 the plain mean of all intervals is 148.19 USD/MWh and the median 24.60). Source: docs/methods/ercot_peak_premium.md, "Metrics"; the mean of 2021 is computed by replication/ercot_peak_premium/from_hourly.py from the same 15-minute prices and is in no table. A quartile, a median and the 99.9th percentile are read from the sorted prices by linear interpolation at position (n - 1) times p over 100. i Default: The default of numpy.percentile. Another convention moves a figure by less than the gap between two neighbouring prices. Source: docs/methods/ercot_peak_premium.md, "Notation". Every interval the source publishes is used: nothing is capped, floored or left out, negative prices and prices at the offer cap included. i Default: No exclusion. February 2021 (Winter Storm Uri) is in 2021's figures: 645 intervals at or above 1,000 USD/MWh at the hub average, and a 99.9th percentile of 9,000.85. Source: docs/methods/ercot_peak_premium.md, "Notation" and "Completeness"; ercot_peak_premium_annual. The price is ERCOT's real-time settlement point price, one value every 15 minutes, as ERCOT settles it. It is not the day-ahead price and not a traded forward. i Default: Real time, each interval counted once whatever the load in it (time-weighted). ERCOT's market monitor reports load-weighted averages by load zone, which run higher: 31.91 USD/MWh for 2024 against a plain mean of 26.83 at the hub average. Source: ERCOT reports NP6-785-ER (yearly history) and NP6-905-CD (the newest days). Potomac Economics, 2024 State of the Market Report for the ERCOT Electricity Markets, May 2025, Table 1, page 5. The page opens on HB_HUBAVG, the average of ERCOT's four trading hubs, and offers each of the six hubs the tables hold. i Default: HB_HUBAVG, the hub of the thesis. The others: HB_BUSAVG (ERCOT's average over the buses of the four hubs), HB_NORTH, HB_SOUTH, HB_WEST, HB_HOUSTON. No load zone and no resource node. A second hub can be set beside the first for the same period ("Beside hub" in the filters); none is by default. Source: Session 403; the hubs are the entities of ercot_peak_premium_annual. A year and a month are ERCOT's own: the local (America/Chicago) year and calendar month in which an interval starts, daylight saving included. i Default: Central time. The day the clocks go forward has 92 intervals and the day they go back 100; only the overnight block changes. Source: docs/methods/ercot_peak_premium.md, "Input" and "Time-of-day blocks". With no year named the page sets the first year held against the newest complete one; a year or a month not yet over is marked partial and is never the default second year. i Default: 2015 against the newest complete year (the thesis's comparison). A year is complete at 35,040 intervals (35,136 in a leap year); a month at its days times 96, four fewer in March and four more in November. Source: Session 403; the month's exact count since session 7507 (before it a month up to an hour short was drawn as complete). Scarcity is counted as an interval at or above 1,000 USD/MWh, a negative price as an interval at or below 0 (an exact zero counts), and the worst-interval multiple is the 99.9th percentile over the median. i Default: 1,000 USD/MWh and 0 USD/MWh. A count is of 15-minute intervals: four make an hour. The multiple is not of the single highest interval: the 99.9th percentile is the price that one interval in a thousand passes, about 35 of a full year's 35,040 (under nine hours) and about 3 of a month's. The highest interval itself is the maximum in the table of the five numbers; its day and hour are not in these tables. Source: docs/methods/ercot_peak_premium.md, "Metrics". The thresholds are the thesis's, not ERCOT's definitions. A price and a ratio are written with two decimals and a count whole; the tables hold six decimals. i Default: A value exactly halfway (37.035, a median between two prices a cent apart) is rounded away from zero: 37.04. Source: Session 7507, which also writes a whole number of dollars with its two decimals (25.00, not 25). How old the tables are is read from the site's catalogue and the tables' header, and the page calls them stale when they were last computed more than three days ago. i Default: Three days. The two tables are computed on the data machine, not in the daily cloud run, which does not hold the 15-minute history. Source: Session 403; warehouse/run_daily.sh and warehouse/metadata/run_status.csv (the cloud run's rows say "inputs absent in CI").
== demand growth decisions
Decisions on this page (each with its default and its source on hover) Growth is measured from 2019: a year's demand over 2019's, less one, in percent. i Default: 2019, the last whole year before the pandemic and the first whole year the warehouse holds for every area. Another base year gives another figure: ERCOT's average demand grew 27.22 percent from 2019 to 2025 and 5.57 percent from 2024 to 2025. Source: Session 97; docs/methods/demand_growth.md. The growth on the year before is in the grid's table year by year. Weather is not removed: a year's demand is what was metered that year. i Default: As metered. A mild year shows less growth than customers added, and one heat wave can set a peak. The adjustment is a second table, on Demand growth, "With the weather taken out". Source: Session 97; docs/methods/demand_growth.md, and docs/methods/demand_weather.md for the adjustment. Demand is EIA's hourly demand of the grid operator's balancing authority: its adjusted demand, and for the Lower 48 its demand. i Default: EIA's "Adjusted demand" for the seven operators; "Demand" for the Lower 48, whose file has no adjusted column. It is demand as the grid sees it: power made behind a customer's meter (rooftop solar) is not in it. In California it counts the power the grid's batteries take to charge (the line on sales below gives the figures). Source: EIA Form EIA-930, Hourly Electric Grid Monitor, the per-area workbooks; public domain. An hour is used when it is held, above zero, within 25 percent of the median of the four hours around it, and between a third of and three times the area's median hour. Nothing is filled here, but EIA's adjusted demand already holds EIA's own estimate for an hour an operator reported nothing for or reported implausibly, and such an hour is used as a reported one. i Default: 25 percent. Since 2019 the test leaves out 38 hours of CAISO, 7 of PJM, 2 of SPP, 1 of the Lower 48 and 12 hours of zero of NYISO. Without it PJM's 2020 peak would be a faulty 224,345 MW. From 2019 to 2025, 351 hours of the seven operators are EIA's estimates (ERCOT 24, CAISO 94, PJM 171, MISO 58, SPP 2, ISO-NE 2), among them MISO's highest hour of 2025, 120,343 MW on 24 July 2025, a day MISO reported nothing: on MISO's own reports its 2025 peak is 118,106 MW, +1.29 percent on 2019 against +3.21 here. Source: Sessions 97, 103 and 118; docs/methods/impossible_hours.md, rule A. Counted again from EIA's workbooks by session 7508. EIA's estimates counted from EIA's workbooks of 3 October 2026 (the hours where the operator's own Demand is blank or another value), docs/reviews/demand-model-audit-2026-11-26.md, finding 1. A whole year is written when at least 95 percent of its hours are used; a month at 95 percent. i Default: 95 percent. Every year and month of every area passes: the fewest is CAISO's 2019, 8,725 of 8,760 hours. The hours used are in the grid's table year by year. Source: Session 97; docs/methods/demand_growth.md, "The rows". Years, months and hours of the day are the grid's own clock time, daylight saving included. i Default: Central time for ERCOT, MISO and SPP; Pacific for CAISO; Eastern for PJM, NYISO, ISO-NE and the Lower 48. An hour is named by its start. Source: Session 97; docs/methods/eia930_demand_growth.md, "Placing values in time". Average demand is the mean of the hours used, in MW. Over a whole year it is the year's energy divided by its hours. i Default: The mean, not the sum: a leap year and a year with hours left out compare with any other. Multiply by the hours in the year for MWh. Source: Session 97. Peak demand is the highest used hour of the year, an average over that hour. A grid operator's own record is measured over minutes and reads higher. i Default: The hourly average. California's highest hour of 2025 is 43,860 MW here, from 18:00 on 21 August; CAISO's own peak that evening is 44,506 MW, an instant at 18:07. The seven grids' records and 2026 summer peaks that EIA gives from the same form are this table's, to EIA's rounding. Source: Session 97. CAISO, California ISO Peak Load History, January 2026. EIA, Today in Energy, "PJM, ERCOT, and SWPP all set peak load records this summer", 15 October 2026. No peak is given for the Lower 48. i Default: None. Its demand is EIA's sum over every balancing authority, faulty hours included, and a faulty hour inside the sum passes the test: its highest hour could be a fault. Source: Session 97; docs/methods/demand_growth.md, "The Lower 48 has no peak". The year so far is 1 January to the end of the newest year's last whole month, compared with the same months of 2019. i Default: The same months of every year, so a partial year is never set against a whole one. The window moves by a month with each monthly build; the page reads its end from the table's header. Source: Session 97; the table's header line "year to date". A month's or an hour's growth is read at the last whole year, even where the newest year already holds that month. i Default: The last whole year. The table writes a month's and an hour's change from 2019 on that year's row only; the newest year's months are in the grid's table year by year, without a growth. Source: Session 97's builder; session 404 for the page. Whether a month the newest year holds should show its own growth is the owner's to rule. California's hours from 1 November 2023 to 2 December 2025 are read one hour earlier than EIA stamps them. i Default: One hour, over that period only; the hour left empty on 2 December 2025 is not filled. Source: Session 82; docs/methods/eia930_caiso_break.md, "The late hours". Eight areas: the seven grid operators' balancing authorities and the Lower 48. A balancing authority is not a state, and not always the operator's whole market. i Default: ERCOT, CAISO, PJM, MISO, SPP, NYISO, ISO-NE and the Lower 48. The large balancing authorities outside the seven (the Tennessee Valley Authority, Southern Company, Bonneville, Duke, Florida Power and Light) are not here. Source: Session 97; EIA Form EIA-930's balancing authorities. Demand at the grid is not sales to customers. EIA's retail sales moved with the Lower 48's demand, and apart from it in California and New England. i Default: From 2019 to 2025: United States retail sales +7.05 percent, the Lower 48's demand here +7.03; Texas +24.20, ERCOT +27.22; New York -1.26, NYISO -2.73; New England +0.14, ISO-NE -2.54; California -2.96, CAISO +4.45. A state is not a balancing authority, and sales leave out line losses. California: EIA's demand counts the power the batteries take to charge, which is not a sale to a customer. Where that charging is held (CAISO's own data, from June 2025): June to December 2025, demand here +2.81 percent on the same months of 2019, the charging 1,690 MW on average, demand less the charging -3.60 percent, retail sales -2.28 percent; January to August 2026, demand +10.75 percent, the charging 2,114 MW, demand less the charging +2.05 percent, retail sales -2.90 percent. The charging is more than all of the growth of June to December 2025 and 81 percent of that of January to August 2026; it does not close 2026's gap to sales. Source: EIA, Electric Power Monthly, data for August 2026, table 5.1 (sales to ultimate customers), and the same form (EIA-861M) by state as held in eia_retail_sales_monthly on 5 November 2026; the percents computed by session 7508. The batteries' charging: CAISO's supply by fuel as held in shoulder_hours_monthly (built 4 October 2026), computed by session 7517 (replication/demand_growth/california_charging.py). The page opens on ERCOT and the whole year's average hour; an address the table does not hold falls back to that and says so. i Default: ERCOT, the whole year's average hour: the table's headline figure. Each grid is drawn at its own latest year. Source: Session 404. Every figure is one row of the table, written as the table holds it: MW whole, percent to two decimals, a growth with its sign. i Default: No arithmetic on the page. A figure the table does not hold reads "not held", with no unit. Source: Sessions 404 and 7508; site/scripts/check-values.mjs reads each figure again from the live set.
