## The three to post first

1. **Post 6, ERCOT's large loads (8,927 MW approved, 3,675 MW served at one moment).** It is the platform's sharpest current lens, AI's demand for power, told with ERCOT's own reported figures rather than a forecast, and the point (approval is more than twice use) is one a reader rarely sees stated. It names the pause of 3 August 2026, so it is current even though the newest report is of 19 June. What a sceptic says: the June report is four months old, and approved MW is not requested MW; the post says both plainly.
2. **Post 9, SPP's 2023 queue (340 of 435 requests withdrawn, 8 operating).** Interconnection queues are the second question everyone asks about new load and new supply, and a cohort counted by where it stands now is rarer than the usual "GW in the queue" headline. SPP is chosen over ERCOT because the ERCOT queue table carries a known queue-date fault until the data machine rebuilds it. What a sceptic says: a 2023 cohort is young, so some of those still waiting may yet build; the post says "have since been withdrawn" and gives the operating count, not a forecast.
3. **Post 4, Texas in the January 2025 cold (47.97 percent above its usual winter peak, the most of six grids).** It shows what only a warehouse can: one measure applied to six grids through the same week. It is also the least expected reading of the ten (Texas is known for summer). What a sceptic says: a baseline of two earlier years is short, and a mild 2023 or 2024 January would inflate the rise; the baseline is stated in the post's first line so the reader can judge.

Why not the others first: posts 1 to 3 are history a reader has seen before (Uri, Elliott, a heat wave), good later as a series on the events pages; post 5 (curtailment) and post 8 (carbon intensity) are seasonal and read best in spring; post 7 (959 intervals at or below zero) and post 10 (what each grid's load paid) are strong second-week posts once the price pages are open.
