## The reader and the five cases

The reader: **a load forecaster at a utility in the Southeast, writing the demand chapter of an integrated resource plan**, who has to set the utility's own growth beside its neighbours' and say what part of it is weather. Cases 4 and 5 are also the ones a fund analyst and an AI lab's energy lead bring to this page. Real places, real years:

1. **Tennessee beside the Carolinas, 2019 to 2025** (`/demand?view=country`, the view as it opens): how much the average and the highest hour of each region grew since 2019, and when each region's highest hour of 2025 fell. The contract's own sample (`docs/state/page_files_regions.md`, section 3, read from `eia930_region_hourly` by session 8000) gives Tennessee's row of 2025: 19,490.4 MW on average, +7.15 percent on 2019, a highest hour of 35,086 MW, +18.66 percent on 2019's, in the hour that began at 13:00 UTC on 22 January 2025 (07:00 in Tennessee). The first thing to read on the real file is whether the page writes that row.
2. **The Tennessee Valley Authority beside Duke Energy Carolinas, the days of 2022** (`/demand?view=country&a=ba-tva&b=ba-duk&days=2022`): the day of most demand and the highest hour of 2022 for each, and how 23 and 24 December 2022 (Winter Storm Elliott, in which both shed load) read in the chart of days: as two days of demand, as a break in the line, or as days whose highest hour was left out by the screen.
3. **Texas, EIA's region, beside ERCOT, the balancing authority, 2019 to 2025** (`/demand?view=country&a=tex&b=ba-erco`): whether the region's figures, the authority's figures on this view and the as-metered view's ERCOT (`/demand?area=ercot`, where the method note's own example reads +27.22 percent for the average hour of 2025 on 2019 and +12.16 for the highest hour) are one figure, and if not, whether the page says why.
4. **The Southeast beside Florida, the summer peak with the weather taken out, 2023 to 2025** (`/demand?view=country&a=se&b=fla&figure=summer&year=2025`): how much of each summer peak's growth on the fit's years the weather explains, what is left, and whether what is left is larger than its give or take. A fund analyst asks the same of the Southeast, where the large new loads of Georgia are.
5. **Arizona Public Service beside the Salt River Project, where the growth is, 2019 to 2025** (`/demand?view=country&a=ba-azps&b=ba-srp`): in which months and hours of the day the demand of the two authorities of the Phoenix area grew, the newest seven days of each, and what the page says where it has no weather view for a single authority. An AI lab's energy lead choosing between the two service areas asks this.

For each case the questions are the same: can the reader get the answer without leaving the page, without a figure that contradicts another page, and without a word they would not know?
