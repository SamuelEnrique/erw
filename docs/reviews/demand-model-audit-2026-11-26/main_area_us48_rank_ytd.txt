== titles
A year's demand is what was metered that year, hot summer and cold snap included. A grid whose 2025 was mild shows less growth than its customers added, and a single heat wave can set a peak. These are differences between years, not a trend line.
Demand at the grid is not sales to customers. EIA's retail sales moved with the Lower 48's demand, and apart from it in California and New England. From 2019 to 2025: United States retail sales +7.05 percent, the Lower 48's demand here +7.03; Texas +24.20, ERCOT +27.22; New York -1.26, NYISO -2.73; New England +0.14, ISO-NE -2.54; California -2.96, CAISO +4.45. A state is not a balancing authority, and sales leave out line losses. California: EIA's demand counts the power the batteries take to charge, which is not a sale to a customer. Where that charging is held (CAISO's own data, from June 2025): June to December 2025, demand here +2.81 percent on the same months of 2019, the charging 1,690 MW on average, demand less the charging -3.60 percent, retail sales -2.28 percent; January to August 2026, demand +10.75 percent, the charging 2,114 MW, demand less the charging +2.05 percent, retail sales -2.90 percent. The charging is more than all of the growth of June to December 2025 and 81 percent of that of January to August 2026; it does not close 2026's gap to sales. Source: EIA, Electric Power Monthly, data for August 2026, table 5.1 (sales to ultimate customers), and the same form (EIA-861M) by state as held in eia_retail_sales_monthly on 5 November 2026; the percents computed by session 7508. The batteries' charging: CAISO's supply by fuel as held in shoulder_hours_monthly (built 4 October 2026), computed by session 7517 (replication/demand_growth/california_charging.py).

An hour is used when its demand is held, above zero and within 25 percent of the median of the four hours around it, since EIA's file holds faulty hours and a faulty high hour would be a year's peak; nothing is filled. A year's average and peak are written when at least 95 percent of its hours are used, a month when 95 percent of its hours are, and a month's hour of the day when 90 percent are. Growth is measured against 2019, the last whole year before the pandemic in the file, and the newest partial year is set against the same months of every earlier year. The Lower 48 has averages and no peak: its demand is EIA's sum over every balancing authority, faulty hours included, and such an hour passes the screen while it would set the peak.
