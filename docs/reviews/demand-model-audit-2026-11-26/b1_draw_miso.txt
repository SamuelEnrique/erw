== titles
A year's demand is what was metered that year, hot summer and cold snap included. A grid whose 2025 was mild shows less growth than its customers added, and a single heat wave can set a peak. These are differences between years, not a trend line.
Demand at the grid is not sales to customers. EIA's retail sales moved with the Lower 48's demand, and apart from it in California and New England. From 2019 to 2025: United States retail sales +7.05 percent, the Lower 48's demand here +7.03; Texas +24.20, ERCOT +27.22; New York -1.26, NYISO -2.73; New England +0.14, ISO-NE -2.54; California -2.96, CAISO +4.45. A state is not a balancing authority, and sales leave out line losses. California: EIA's demand counts the power the batteries take to charge, which is not a sale to a customer. Where that charging is held (CAISO's own data, from June 2025): June to December 2025, demand here +2.81 percent on the same months of 2019, the charging 1,690 MW on average, demand less the charging -3.60 percent, retail sales -2.28 percent; January to August 2026, demand +10.75 percent, the charging 2,114 MW, demand less the charging +2.05 percent, retail sales -2.90 percent. The charging is more than all of the growth of June to December 2025 and 81 percent of that of January to August 2026; it does not close 2026's gap to sales. Source: EIA, Electric Power Monthly, data for August 2026, table 5.1 (sales to ultimate customers), and the same form (EIA-861M) by state as held in eia_retail_sales_monthly on 5 November 2026; the percents computed by session 7508. The batteries' charging: CAISO's supply by fuel as held in shoulder_hours_monthly (built 4 October 2026), computed by session 7517 (replication/demand_growth/california_charging.py).
The year so far is January to October of each year, so 2026 is compared with the same months of 2019.
MISO, 2025 on 2024: +2.96 percent, a calendar year's average over the year before's. The All grids tab of the datacenter cost page (/cost-of-power?view=allgrids) gives +2.76 percent for November 2025 to October 2026 on the twelve months before, from the same table: another period, not another figure for this one.
The grid's own clock time, daylight saving included: Central for ERCOT, MISO and SPP, Pacific for CAISO, Eastern for PJM, NYISO, ISO-NE and the Lower 48. An hour is named by its start: 17:00 is the hour from 17:00 to 18:00, which the operators' own files call hour ending 18.
From EIA-930's hourly demand. An hour is used when its demand is above zero and within 25 percent of the median of the four hours around it: EIA's file holds faulty hours, and a faulty high hour would otherwise be a year's peak. A year is written when at least 95 percent of its hours are used; its average is the mean of those hours. Nothing is filled here. A grid operator's hours are EIA's Adjusted demand, which already holds EIA's own estimate for an hour the operator reported nothing for or reported implausibly, and such an hour is used as a reported one: MISO's highest hour of 2025 (24 July 2025, a day MISO reported nothing) is EIA's estimate.
The last row compares January to October of 2026 with January to October of 2019: its growth is against those months, not against the whole of 2019.
Each cell is one hour of the day in one month: the average demand of that hour over the month in 2025, against the same in 2019. Red is growth, dark grey a fall; the deeper, the larger. One hot or mild month colors a whole row, which is the weather and not a trend.
