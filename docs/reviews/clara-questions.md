# Questions for Clara: California reliability v1

Energy Research Warehouse (ERW), session 58. Ten questions for someone who worked at the California Energy Commission.

They are about `/grid/caiso`'s new "Reliability" section and `/events/caiso-heat-2022` (method: `docs/methods/california_reliability.md`). The aim is the grid-stress tool: what the state needs to see before a tight evening, and how early. Each question names the part of the section it would change.

1. **Lead time** (the whole section). When the state prepares for a tight evening, how many hours or days ahead does it need a warning? Which of CAISO's signals actually set things in motion: the day-ahead EEA Watch, a Flex Alert, the RMO notice? Today the section is a record, after the fact; a forecast view would need this.
2. **The measure of tightness** ("How tight was it"). We rank days by the peak hour of demand served and show the evening ramp. Would the CEC rank them by something else, such as the margin of available supply over demand plus reserves, or net demand after solar and wind? Which figure did the state watch on those evenings?
3. **Available supply** (the missing column). CAISO's Today's Outlook publishes "available resources", which we do not hold yet. Is that the right supply figure to set against peak demand, or does the state use its own, such as the CEC's or CPUC's resource adequacy view? Should imports be counted, and at what point in the day?
4. **The evening ramp's hours** (the ramp column). We measure it from the mean of 12:00 to 15:00 to the highest hour of 17:00 to 21:00, Pacific. Do these windows match how the state thinks about the net peak, or should they move with the season and with sunset?
5. **Batteries at the peak** (the battery column). Since 2025-08-24 batteries have supplied a median of about 23 percent of the evening peak's demand. Is the share of demand the right lens, or would the state rather see the batteries' energy left at the start of the evening (state of charge), which decides whether they last through it?
6. **Which notices matter** (the timeline). We count days with a notice of each type, as CAISO's own summary does. Which notice types are signal and which are routine? RMO, for example, runs for weeks some years: is it a stress signal, or maintenance planning?
7. **Heat against scarcity** (the September 2022 event study). Controlling for temperature at Sacramento and Los Angeles removes about five sixths of the extra demand. Is a two-station average a fair stand-in for California's weather on a heat day? Which stations, or population-weighted index, does the CEC use?
8. **The demand that was not served** (the event page's caveat). Demand here is demand served. On a day of conservation calls or demand response, how much load was held back? Does the state publish an estimate of the conservation response to a Flex Alert that we could set beside the peak?
9. **Regions** (the timeline's regions). Many notices name a region (Southern CA, Northern CA, a local area) rather than the whole ISO. For the state's purposes, should the section separate statewide stress from local transmission stress?
10. **What the tool should show first** (the section's order). If a CEC analyst opened `/grid/caiso` at 14:00 on a hot day, what is the first thing they would want to see? A forecast margin for the evening, the current notice, the battery fleet's charge, the import picture? What would make it worth opening every summer afternoon?
