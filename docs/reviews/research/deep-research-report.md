# Energy Data, Tools, and Analysis Landscape

A wide array of sources serve energy/infrastructure data and analysis today:

- **Open data (free/government):**  The DOE/EIA provides extensive official statistics (generation by fuel, capacity, etc.).  All major U.S. grid operators (ERCOT, CAISO, PJM, etc.) publish near-real-time load and generation data on their dashboards.  NOAA and NREL offer weather and renewable resource data (solar insolation, wind speeds).  Local utilities may provide usage maps.  For example, the ElectricChoice *Data Center Tracker* (free) aggregates public data to report ~3,525 U.S. data centers totaling 192 TWh/yr (≈4.7% of U.S. electricity).  These free sources are used by utilities and analysts, but they require manual collation.

- **Commercial data/services:**  Energy/commodity firms like **Wood Mackenzie, S&P Global Platts, Rystad Energy (NRGStream)**, Enverus (formerly DrillingInfo), IHS, BloombergNEF, etc., sell detailed datasets and analysis on fuel prices, power plant pipelines, interconnection queues, etc.  Their pricing is very high: Cleanview (a startup) notes that legacy power project datasets run roughly **$40K–80K per year**.  In contrast, new entrants like **Cleanview** (a startup) charge ~$9K–$10K/yr for API access to U.S. data-center project pipelines and power projects.  Cleanview's "Geography of Compute" report, for instance, maps ~445 GW of planned U.S. data-center capacity by state/county. Other paid tools include Energy-Intelligence software (OSI PI), power market terminals (e.g. ABB Ventyx), and specialized trackers (e.g. Electric Choice above, or data-center pipelines).

- **Academic and NGO analyses:**  Think tanks and labs also publish energy insights.  Princeton's Jesse Jenkins (Princeton) and others produce papers and models on U.S. grid decarbonization; Argonne's Chris Dunlap has preprints on AI grid flexibility (cited by Data Center Knowledge).  RMI, EPRI, NREL, MIT, etc., release reports and maps (e.g. transmission buildouts, solar potential).  These are typically free or low-cost, though not tailored to AI-specific questions.

- **Independent analysts and newsletters:**  A small number of analysts/bloggers have built reputations.  For example, Brian Potter's *Construction Physics* Substack shares detailed grid and infrastructure "fact" posts (one analysis notes "the US has about 10,000 times as much energy storage capacity for hydrocarbons as it does for grid-scale electricity").  Others include Dan Yergin (EnergyIQ), Shayle Kann (raveny.org), or Electricity Maps (for carbon intensity).  Industry newsletters (Grid Strategies newsletter, BloombergNEF Clearing, RMI Rocky Mountain Institute's emails) are followed by investors and utilities.  AI-lab and data-center insiders often read energy-focused media (Data Center Knowledge, Utility Dive).  For instance, Data Center Knowledge journalist Shane Snider is widely cited by OpenAI/Anthropic press.

**What's missing:**  Despite many sources, there's no single, integrated public platform that ties AI compute demand to detailed grid data.  Existing tools tend to isolate pieces (e.g. Cleanview or ElectricChoice cover projects, ISO sites cover real-time flows, legacy vendors cover markets).  Notably, research has highlighted *coordination gaps*: as one expert notes, flexible AI loads could help the grid but "the hardest part is coordination" – independent data centers reacting to signals can cause unpredictable transfers (an unsolved aggregation problem).  In practice, labs and investors still lack a turnkey "AI energy dashboard."  They often have to mash together open ISO data, EIA stats, and sporadic reports.  In summary, lots of raw data exists, but few user-friendly analytical tools specifically address *where* to site AI compute or *how* to model its power usage – exactly the gap the user's open data warehouse could fill.

# "Playbook" from Similar Successes

Looking at people who built influence with published analysis reveals some common strategies:

- **Dylan Patel (SemiAnalysis):**  Patel launched his first Substack post on *May 22, 2020* (his 24th birthday).  He posted regularly (initially free) on semiconductor supply chains and GPU demand, coining concepts like "GPU-rich vs GPU-poor" that spread in the industry.  Over three years he grew SemiAnalysis to ~260,000 subscribers and ~$20M revenue by 2024, funded by selling deep reports to VCs and hyperscalers.  A key milestone was the InferenceX report (2025) – a data-centric analysis of GPU availability – which NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang publicly cited at GTC 2026.  The timeline illustrates: *start with a niche newsletter, publish often (semiweekly+), and gradually sell premium analyses once an audience is built*.  Patel's success underscores persistence (he had no funding initially) and producing definitive benchmark reports that the industry cites.

- **Brian Potter (Construction Physics):**  Potter's Substack (started ~2021) focuses on data-driven essays about infrastructure.  He posts regularly (weekly) and compiles "insights" series (e.g. "50 Things I've Learned…") demonstrating subject-matter depth.  For instance, his public analyses include non-obvious facts (like the 10,000× energy storage gap), which attract shares in the energy community.  He monetized later via paid subscriptions and consulting.  Potter's approach: build credibility by delivering rigorously researched posts consistently, long before seeking to be "hired."

- **Packy McCormick (Not Boring):**  McCormick's *Not Boring* newsletter (launched ~2019) achieved rapid growth through weekly long-form essays on tech business/finance.  He often combined storytelling with data charts, building to ~150,000+ subscribers.  His popularity led him to start a VC fund.  While Packy's content is broad, a similar tactic applies: pick a clear thematic lens (AI+energy), publish analysis consistently (daily/weekly posts), and engage readers on platforms (LinkedIn, Substack).

- **Ben Thompson (Stratechery):**  Thompson's Stratechery (2013–present) pioneered the paid newsletter model in tech analysis.  He writes two posts per week (one free, one paid) dissecting industry trends with clear frameworks.  Thompson advises consistency and a gradual monetization.  Notably, he also maintains a public feed (Twitter) to amplify his work.

- **Jesse Jenkins and others:**  In energy, academics like Jesse Jenkins or Ed Hirs have long published studies and blog posts.  Jenkins in particular grew influence by authoring technical reports and appearing in media on decarbonization.  One can emulate this by making a clear research agenda (e.g. "how AI demand affects grid reliability") and publishing both short updates and deep dives.

**Key takeaways:** All these examples started *at zero with free publishing*, then built audience trust by *repeated, high-quality analysis*.  They became known by having signature research pieces (Patel's InferenceX, Potter's "50 Things" essays, Thompson's daily updates).  They then used that reputation to land bigger roles or revenue.  Common advice from them: be relentless and transparent (Patel even said he 'acted like he was already famous' in early days), focus on unique data-driven insights, and leverage networks by sharing work widely (LinkedIn, Twitter, newsletters).

# Roadmap: What to Build and Publish (Next 6 Months)

**Top priority: Launch and showcase your open energy data warehouse.**  This is a unique asset.  In month 1–2, make a **public release**: document the data (sources, refresh cadence), publish an API or simple web interface, and write an introductory post on LinkedIn announcing it.  Emphasize that this "open, citable US energy data hub" enables faster analysis of national power systems.  (For inspiration, note Cleanview's model: they position their data as essential for anyone planning data centers, and price it transparently.)

**First LinkedIn posts (tentative plan):**
1. **Intro/teaser:** Announce the data warehouse project. Include a neat infographic (e.g. US map of generation by state) generated from your data. End with a question or call for feedback.
2. **Insight from data:** Post a chart or stat extracted from the warehouse. Example: "How much U.S. electricity each state adds per year (and how it compares to projected AI load)" with source citation from your data and/or EIA.
3. **Compute vs. Grid:** A graphic overlaying major current/plan AI data-center sites on a transmission map, showing supply shortages. Use your data plus open transmission data (e.g. CAISO/TX interconnect queues). Show *where* AI hubs cluster relative to power flows.
4. **Cost/Siting analysis:** e.g. "Projected cost to power 1 MW of new AI compute in [State X]" using your fuel price data and state tariffs. This shows technical skill and thinking.
5. **Policy angle:** A brief commentary linking an energy policy development (e.g. FERC rule, or a moratorium on data centers in California) to AI supply.
6–10. Repeat cycles: each time present a new **data-driven insight** or small model. Examples: "Renewable laggards: which new AI sites will face a thermal power crunch?" or "Battery backup needed for [X] MW flexible load."  Ideally include references (e.g. cite DOE reliability studies, EIA stats) to show rigor.

**First long-form pieces:** (publish as LinkedIn articles or Substack posts)
- **"The State of the U.S. Energy System (through an AI lens)."**  A comprehensive report (~2,000+ words) that maps out U.S. generation by source, transmission bottlenecks, current data-center buildouts, and identifies key gaps/risks for AI load. Use charts (from your warehouse) and cite authoritative data (EIA, DOE, FERC). This establishes you as knowledgeable about the whole system.
- **"Power, Costs, and Siting of Next-Gen AI Compute."**  A detailed analysis of where AI data centers might go. Model power cost differences (LCOE) across regions and fuel types, consider carbon intensity, and factor in interconnection queue wait times. The centerpiece could be a tool or map (built with your data) showing optimal sites for given compute power.
- **"Flexible AI Loads and the Grid: Myths vs. Reality."**  A deep dive on the idea of demand flexibility (inspired by Dunlap/Das). Show modeling of how delayable AI workload can participate in DR programs. Use your data to simulate a case study (e.g. 500 MW campus using storage + scheduling). This plays directly to Anthropic/OpenAI interests (as they explore this in Project Camellia).

**Newsletter:** If you choose one, it should be weekly or bi-weekly, summarizing "energy meets AI" news and your analysis. Name it something like **"AI & Energy Dispatch"**. Each issue could have 3–5 short sections: industry headlines (e.g. regulatory news, Intel fab announcements, grid reports) and then 1–2 charts from your own work. Feature the open data warehouse repeatedly. A newsletter helps turn casual readers into subscribers (critical mass by ~6 months shows momentum).

**Differentiators vs. peers:**  Many Stanford students could analyze EIA data or write about climate. But few (if any) have:
- An **open, automated national energy dataset** (yours). Most analysts rely on scraped PDFs or paywalled data. Publicize this as a public good and cite it.
- A deep focus on **AI compute as lens**. Emphasize analysis specifically for AI loads (e.g. modeling GPU farms, inference distribution) – not just generic climate or grid analysis.
- The combination of energy *and* finance: maybe one analysis could be "Estimated capital needed to expand U.S. grid by 50 GW (to meet AI demand) and where that money might come from." This leverages your MBA background.
- Frequent, insight-driven content on LinkedIn, which few energy people do.

**Avoid waste of time:**  Don't publish scattershot posts without data, and avoid purely theoretical rants.  Unless it uses your data or original models, an article won't stand out.  Also, be selective in coding tools: building generic ML models on this data without a clear AI energy angle might not impress.  Focus on concrete analyses (site/cost/volume) over vague commentary.

**On the data warehouse's role:**  It will be the factual backbone of your brand.  Publicizing it is high-impact (it's unique content that others can cite, a fact many analysts emphasize).  But *alone* it won't attract recruiters: it must power stories and tools.  Plan to put a web interface or API so others (including recruiters or journalists) can explore it.  For example, build a mini-website with interactive maps (like Cleanview's trackers).  Also consider writing a technical "How It Works" post about your pipeline (short-term: just as a report, later maybe academic style).

**Suggested content order (by impact):**
1. **Open data release + introductory analysis (≈Month 1):** Big impact.
2. **Long-form pieces (months 2–4):** Each cited by peers, recruiter impressions.
3. **LinkedIn posts (ongoing weekly):** Builds network buzz; the first 10 should mix charts and commentary as above.
4. **Newsletter launch (month 2 or 3):** Captures audience.
5. **Interactive tools/demos (month 4–6):** E.g. a map showing real-time LMP prices vs projected AI load; or a simple browser app querying your data (leveraging your AI skills).
6. **Open-source model/code:** If you build an AI-forecast model or data pipeline, consider releasing on GitHub (could enhance credibility).

Each piece should be *connected to verifiable data/quotes*. For instance, your posts could cite DOE/ISO rules or industry examples (as we cite here) to show research rigor.  This signals to recruiters that you think like an analyst, not just a blogger.

# Risks, Constraints, and Timing

- **Data licensing & redistribution:**  Using public data (EIA, ISOs, GIS shapefiles, etc.) is generally safe, but proprietary datasets (from vendors or map APIs) can't be republished freely.  Ensure all sources in your warehouse allow reuse or are compiled via API (e.g. EIA API or open interconnection queues).  If you ingest any paywalled data, check license terms.  Many data firms (WoodMac, IHS) forbid republishing; if you rely on them behind the scenes, you cannot share that output.  Sticking to open sources (and citing them) avoids legal traps.

- **Competitors:**  Cleanview (and older services like *NewProjectMedia*/WoodMack) are directly aiming at the same market.  Cleanview's ~$9K/yr data product shows the demand for curated pipelines.  If they (or someone like CBRE's *DatacenterHawk*) expand free analysis, it could reduce visibility for yours.  However, competing head-on with their product isn't necessary: focus on unique angles (AI-specific metrics, or economic modeling) rather than raw pipelines.  Also watch out for in-house initiatives (e.g. DOE or NGOs launching dashboards); aim to complement, not duplicate them.

- **Hiring cycles:**  Tech/data roles at AI labs can appear year-round but often follow project funding or data-center build schedules.  Anthropic/OpenAI may open roles as new sites are planned (watch their property acquisitions and resource reports).  VC hiring often doesn't follow a strict schedule – but many firms finalize new funds or teams late in the year.  Given your 12-month timeline, start outreach early.  By publishing now (late 2026) you may be noticed for roles or internships in early 2027.  Also consider aligning with Stanford career services: some VCs hire summer associates, so having visibility by winter 2026 could catch recruiter attention.

- **Common pitfalls:**  From the stories above, avoid: (a) **Irregular posting** – consistency builds audience. (b) **Paywall too soon** – start free to grow followers (Dylan didn't charge until well after launch). (c) **Lack of focus** – define your unique niche (you chose "AI's demand lens"). (d) **Underestimating effort** – building these projects and writing quality pieces is time-consuming; plan a manageable schedule (Packy and Thompson post like clockwork).  (e) **Over-optimism on virality** – even great posts often take weeks to gain traction; be patient and keep publishing.

- **Technical risks:**  Automating data pipelines can break if APIs change (watch EIA or ISO sites).  Real-time data modeling (e.g. grid simulations) may require more detail or compute than anticipated.  Keep prototypes simple initially (e.g. monthly data analysis before attempting true real-time).

**Ranking of recommendations (most to least impact):**
1. **Make the data warehouse public (with tools)** – this is the foundation asset, so highest impact.
2. **Publish deep analysis pieces** – long-form, data-backed articles that recruiters might bookmark.
3. **Weekly LinkedIn posts with visuals** – builds visible reputation among peers/HR scouts.
4. **Newsletter (if you commit to it)** – demonstrates consistency and gathers a following.
5. **Build demo tools or APIs** – shows technical chops to employers (e.g. a small web app).
6. **Networking (sharing your work)** – e.g. reach out to energy/AI folks with your reports, but only after you have solid content.
7. **Attending events/speaking** – lower priority in 6 months, but possible once you have content to share.

Overall, the evidence suggests focusing first on creating unique, data-driven content (blogs/reports) and the open data tool, since these directly showcase relevant expertise.  Fluff or generic commentary would not stand out in this evidence-driven niche.
