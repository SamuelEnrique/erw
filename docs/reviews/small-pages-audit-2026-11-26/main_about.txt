About The Energy Research Warehouse (ERW) is the live, citable record of the US energy system: prices, flows, projects, deals and policy across power, natural gas, oil, nuclear, renewables, storage and transmission, with the global prices and events that move US markets. AI's demand for power is the sharpest current lens on that system, not its boundary. It is built as a Stanford independent study. Its design follows the Item Response Warehouse (IRW), whose owner allowed the ERW to copy its infrastructure and documents: one connector per source, a small number of standard table shapes so that sources can be joined and compared, a validator every table must pass, a provenance header on every table naming the source report and the time it was retrieved, and a versioned store of record on Redivis that only a human releases. Every table carries a license from its sources: a table is public only if all of its sources allow republication, and a table derived from other tables takes the most restrictive license among them. Tables licensed for internal use only, such as PJM market data, stay in the warehouse and are never shown on this site. How the digest and ERW's Roundup are made The periodicals' method : how each part of the Daily Digest and the Weekly Roundup is chosen, each rule with its ruling, each figure's key and its tracer, and when and to whom each issue is sent. Stories. The ERW reads energy news feeds (the outlets are listed in the source registry) and keeps each story's title, summary and link, never its body. Titles and summaries are the outlets' text, kept for scoring and linking; they are not republished. Scoring. A model (the newest Sonnet-class Claude model the API lists) scores every story against a fixed rubric ( warehouse/news/rubric.md ): significance from 0 to 10 across the whole energy industry, a sector, a region, and a one-line reason. Stories about the same event are grouped into one cluster, and clusters are ranked by their highest significance. The ranking decides the order; the score itself is not shown. Headlines. The model writes each cluster's headline, using only words and figures in the stories' titles, summaries and scored fields; a headline's numbers are checked against them. The line under each headline is the story's own scored reason, with its sources linked. The line under the headline. It says the consequence in one sentence: no adjective of the list (massive, unprecedented, significant, major, critical, key, huge), no figure the headline already gives, a stop before the figures and the sources. The scorer is asked for that and the brief checks it: an adjective is cut, a second sentence is dropped, a figure the headline holds is taken out where the sentence still reads, and a line that cannot be mended is left out rather than invented. Sums of money are written one way, “USD 45 billion”. Sections. An issue has its top 10 stories, up to three more for each group of sectors, and AI and power; a story is shown in one section only, the first that takes it. AI and power takes up to five stories that the scorer rated 7 or more for AI's demand for power and 5 or more for significance, so commentary and minor items are not in it. One event once. The scorer groups stories only within one batch, so one event can reach an issue as two clusters. Two clusters are one event when they name a party in common, are filed in the same sector, and either their lead titles share most of their words or they share an amount (a sum of USD 1 million or more, or a capacity of 10 MW or more). The lower-ranked of the two is left out before the sections are filled, so the top 10 still holds ten stories. A story is also dropped when its headline or any of its links is already in the issue, and the top stories are numbered again; the run log names each story dropped. One topic, one item. The top 10 holds at most one item a topic: a topic is the scorer's sector, or two sectors whose stories name a party in common and share two words of their titles. A later story of a topic already in the ten folds under that item as “Also on this topic”, with its publisher's title and its outlet, at most three an item; past three it waits for the sections below, so no story is lost. The Roundup's weekend stories and its five of the week follow the same rule. Not the same story twice in a week. A story that a digest of the seven days before printed is left out: a link in common, or a party named again with either an amount that issue printed or most of its headline's words. It comes back only when it brings a sum of money none of those issues printed, such as a price disclosed after the deal. The Roundup leaves out, in the same way, the stories the previous week's Roundup printed. Links. A top story links up to three of its sources and a story in the other sections its first, each address once. The ERW follows a Google News link once to reach the outlet; when it cannot, the link stays a Google News address and says so, “(via Google News)”, in the digest, the Roundup and the digest's email. The link's text is the outlet's name from the feed, never the address; the email's text part names the outlet alone where the address is Google's. An outlet whose stories ask for a license (the news index's hold since 21 October 2026, read from the license file) is named with no link, so the item shows the outlet and the title only. Numbers. Every number in the numbers section is read from the ERW's tables through its Python package, and each names its table in the section's footnotes. A day-ahead average is the mean of the 24 hourly prices of the grid's own operating day, rounded half up to cents. The section opens with what moved most: the five largest moves in percent among the main hubs' day-ahead averages against the day before and EIA's spot closes against the close before them, each with its level and its change, and a reason only when a scored story of the day names that price; the move is the price board's.A price held for a license or paused is not ranked and says so, as are TTF and JKM, which are not held.The table carries the day before and the change; the closes say how old they are; US48 demand's peak is set against the same weekday a year before where the warehouse holds it. The short summary above them is written by the model under a literal check: every number in it must appear in the section, or the summary is regenerated once and otherwise left out. On this site each figure carries the table and key it came from: scripts/check-values.mjs compares it with the live set, and scripts/check_digest.py traces every Digest of the archive to the warehouse. Since 21 October 2026 a figure from a table whose publisher asks for permission reads “license needed”, as it does on every page; so does the summary when it names one, and so do the stories, which come from the news index. Schedule. The digest covers the 24 hours before it is written, Monday to Friday, by the daily run that starts at 14:00 UTC; there is no weekend issue. A day without a digest, before it is written or when none was, says so on its page and shows no other day's. ERW's Roundup covers an ISO week (Monday to Sunday, UTC), opens with the weekend's top stories, and is written and sent on Sundays at 23:00 UTC (4 PM Pacific). It was called Energy Week, on Mondays, until session 23. Chart of the day. Since 13 October 2026 each digest carries a chart drawn by code, with no model and no person choosing it: the strongest flag of the day's scan of the warehouse that no digest has shown ( how the chart is chosen ). Since 22 October 2026 the candidates are ordered first by how much each belongs to the day's top ten stories (the sector the candidate's table belongs to, and the words its fact, table and series share with a story's title, reason and parties; the top story weighs ten times the tenth), and the sentence under the chart says which story it follows. Its figures are its table's, as its record holds them. Fun fact and chart of the week. Each digest ends with a fun fact: always an energy fact first (a number from the warehouse, a piece of energy history, or a quirk of how the system works), sometimes with a second half. A number must match the warehouse exactly, and a history or word-origin claim must be found, word for word, in a stored copy of its source (Wikipedia or EIA), in the source's own words; a fact that fails is dropped and the digest ships without one. The Roundup carries the chart of the week, picked by a fixed rule from the Automated Analysis templates ( /research ); since 22 October 2026 the section says which of the measures that moved in the week is most related to the week's top stories, with its finding, when it is not the one drawn. The week in five numbers. The Roundup opens with five figures, each traced to its table: the week's biggest price move in percent among the main hubs' weekly means and EIA's closes a week apart; the US48 peak with its hour; the week's largest deal by US dollars from the deals table, by MW when none states dollars; the largest datacenter load first reported in the week; and the headline figure of the week's chart. A figure the warehouse does not hold is said missing. The highest real-time price of the week says its interval length, since the grids' series differ in it. The platform The ERW is the data layer of an energy intelligence platform of 31 tools for enthusiasts, investors, researchers, traders and educators. Today 25 of them have a working data layer (6 complete, 19 partial), 2 are not built and 4 are planned. The list, with each tool's tables and gaps, is docs/platform-tools.md . Ask the warehouse. The question-answering tool states no number a query of the warehouse did not return: every figure of an answer is checked against what its tools read, in its unit, and an answer that fails twice says the warehouse does not hold that figure ( its method ). Its evaluation, last asked on 23 October 2026: 19 of 30 hard questions on the economics pages right, graded under the license hold of 21 October (17 of 30 on 20 October), and 24 of 30 of a second set a trading, a finance and a policy lead would ask; most of the wrong answers state the right figure in another unit or from another table, and wait on rulings on units and on how the questions are scored. The site Economics Price board : Every price held on one dense screen: power by hub and zone, gas, crude, products, retail fuel and electricity, coal, metals, rates and credits, each with its moves, trend and one-year range, the spreads, and a workbench to compare any two series. Prices : Every public ISO hub and zone, the newest real-time interval and the day-ahead hour, and each hub's last 30 days; and, on the ERCOT tab, how ERCOT real-time prices spread across the day, by hub and year since 2015. Supply and trade : Is the market tighter or looser than last week, last year and normal for the season: gas storage, crude and product stocks, production, refining, trade, fuel burned for power and positioning, each against its five-year range. Power contracts Severance tax : State production taxes on a month of oil, gas or condensate in Texas, Louisiana and New Mexico: the base rate, the reduced rates and exemptions, and the savings, each rule cited; and, on the Lease tab, the same calculator for every well and month of a lease file, computed in the browser: nothing is sent. Local impact : Jobs, earnings and state and local taxes of a wind, solar, gas or geothermal plant, year by year from its construction to the end of its operation, with their present value: two scenarios side by side. What a battery earns : What a grid battery of your size and duration earned in ERCOT and CAISO from energy and ancillary services together, by year and by stream, and whether it covers its debt, with and without a contract. What a generator earns : What a merchant solar, wind, battery or gas peaker asset earns month by month at an ISO's main hub, its bad months and stress days, and whether it covers its debt. Cost of power : What a MWh costs to buy at each ISO's main hub, weighted by when the grid uses it; when power is cheap by hour of day; cost against carbon; and what a large load pays for energy. What a battery saves a customer The shoulder hours Grid The grid : One grid at a time (ERCOT, CAISO, PJM, MISO, NYISO, ISO-NE, SPP or the Lower 48): what it is doing now, the network of balancing authorities and the power they trade, the energy mix now and since 2001, how much CO2 the power carries, its exchange with its neighbours, and a question box. Demand growth : How much more power each grid uses than in 2019, by year, month and hour of the day, and the same growth with the weather taken out; and, on the Consumption tab, electricity sold by state and sector, and where industrial and commercial load grows fastest. Curtailment : Wind and solar output curtailed, by grid, by day, month and hour, as a share of available output; where power is priced under USD 5 per MWh, by hub and zone; and what the curtailed energy was worth to a load or a battery. Infrastructure Project map : Every EIA generator and ISO queue position on one US map, with filters; and, on the Resources tab, the natural resource itself: wind, sun, geothermal heat, oil and gas basins and plays, hydropower, biomass and offshore wind areas, each from its publisher at its own grain, with the plants, the queue and the datacenters laid over it. Datacenter tracker : Datacenter facilities from the news, nine operators' site lists and the ISO queues: operator, place, MW, status. Storage : The US battery fleet by ISO, state and planned year, and how the batteries charge and discharge each hour and day; on the build-out tab, how much battery storage each US grid has built, of what duration, how it compares with solar, and what is planned; on the owners tab, operating and planned storage by the company that reports each plant: MW, MWh, average duration, and the largest owners. Deals : PPAs, acquisitions, financings and supply deals from the news, with sources: as a sortable table, and on the tracker by type, technology, grid, counterparty and year; and, on the Companies tab, the energy companies the ERW has found and sourced: stage, raised, location, founders and a confidence score, from Thesis Builder runs and the parties of the deals. Companies : Every company the warehouse names: EIA's operators, owners and utilities, ERCOT's queue, the NRC's licensees and the policy agencies, each with its plants, plans, queue positions and the rows that name it. Capital flows : Project finance, acquisitions, debt and equity raises in the deal tracker, by quarter, segment and the kind of company that brings the money, with the share that discloses its size. Analysis Research : Ask for an analysis, today's chart and this week's, and the findings library: one card a screen, by grid and topic, each with its data, its Stata do-file and its Python file. Ask : Ask the warehouse a question; every number in the answer comes from a table it read. Events : What happened to a grid during a major event, day by day, against the same days of earlier years: Winter Storm Uri, COVID-19 in the seven ISO grids, the August 2020 heat in CAISO, Winter Storm Elliott and the summer 2023 heat in ERCOT. Policy : Energy rules, proposed rules and notices from the Federal Register and agency news, scored, with impact reads of the significant ones. Thesis Builder : Type a niche and get a sourced investment map of it: scope and definitions, trends, the company landscape, the deal funnel, the pipeline, capital, incumbents, risks and policy, every figure with its source. Games Home battery game : Run a home battery through a real day of ERCOT prices: charge cheap, sell dear, answer the fleet call, and see what perfect foresight would have earned. The tour : Twelve stops for a first visit, about eight minutes, one page each with Back and Next: the three tools, the grid, research, the price board and the digest, then the whole country: its regions, a storm across it, demand, trade and the resource map. What is on a bill : Five real electricity bills built line by line from the tariffs (PG&E, SCE and SDG&E in California; Oncor and CenterPoint in Texas), and how much of each is the wholesale price of energy. Problem sets : Five sets of five questions for a class: know your grid; prices and your bill; when the grid broke; storage and taxes; networks and money. Should your family get an EV? : For a middle-schooler and their parents: what driving costs in your state on gasoline and on electricity, payback and net present value, and every number with its source, ready to print. Glossary ISO Independent system operator: the organization that runs a region's power grid and wholesale electricity market. ERCOT Electric Reliability Council of Texas: the ISO for most of Texas. CAISO California Independent System Operator: the ISO for most of California. NYISO New York Independent System Operator: the ISO for New York State. MISO Midcontinent Independent System Operator: the ISO for 15 states from Minnesota to Louisiana. SPP Southwest Power Pool: the ISO for the central plains, from North Dakota to Oklahoma and the Texas Panhandle. ISO-NE ISO New England: the ISO for the six New England states. PJM PJM Interconnection: the ISO for 13 mid-Atlantic and Midwest states and DC. The ERW has no licensed PJM price data. EIA U.S. Energy Information Administration: the federal statistics agency for energy; most ERW tables come from it. DAM Day-ahead market: the auction, held the day before, that sets an hourly price for each hour of the next day. RTM Real-time market: the market that balances the grid as it runs, with a price every 5 or 15 minutes. LMP Locational marginal price: the price of one more MWh of power at one place on the grid, in USD/MWh. HSL High Sustained Limit: the output a wind or solar resource reports it could sustain, given the weather, in ERCOT's data. PADD Petroleum Administration for Defense District: one of the five regions EIA uses for oil statistics. RPM Reliability Pricing Model: PJM's capacity market, which pays for power plants to be available years ahead. Code, data standard and session logs: github.com/SamuelEnrique/erw .
